Why an Observability Platform?

For more than a decade, observability has run on interrupt-driven operations: find an engineer, ask, wait. That worked when the estate was small. It does not scale to thousands of hosts, hundreds of applications, and four monitoring tools — and it quietly costs us every day.

Today — how it has run for a decade

Every request is a conversation

Anyone needing an alert or an answer pings a platform engineer, who stops, does the work by hand, and moves to the next interruption. The queue is really a person — requesters wait days for minutes of actual change.

Documentation drifts

Inventories and runbooks are written apart from the systems they describe — stale the week they are written. Nobody trusts them, so people go back to pinging the engineer, which is how the cycle sustains itself.

Approvals and audit are informal

Hand-made changes on a monitoring tool leave no reviewable record of who asked, who approved, and what exactly changed. The audit story depends on memory rather than records.

Platform engineersCapacity spent on repetitive toil instead of platform engineering.
Requesting teamsDays on a queue for what is minutes of work.
The bankAudit risk, and an inventory that lives in a few people's heads.
And manual work has a scaling law: a human applying config by hand will, over enough repetitions, make errors — accumulated across years they become operational drift: what is actually configured no longer matches what anyone believes is configured. More volume means more hands-on changes means more drift, while the team absorbing it stays fixed and small.

What it returns

Savings — simple to state, easy to baseline

monthly savings = requests/month × engineer-minutes per request × loaded rate
  + requester wait-time cost avoided  + audit-preparation effort avoided

Illustrative: at 100 alert requests a month averaging 45 engineer-minutes each, self-service returns ~75 engineer-hours a month — roughly half an FTE — before counting requesters' waiting time or audit preparation. A measured baseline (one quarter of ticket data) turns this from estimate into fact.

And the benefits money doesn't fully capture

  • Drift eliminated — the approved config is applied exactly, identically, every time
  • Audit by construction — requester, approver, and diff permanently on record
  • "Believed monitored" = "actually monitored" — a trustworthy catalog closes the gap that causes avoidable incidents
  • Reduced key-person risk — the process lives in git and the Portal, not in heads
  • No new license spend — open-source Backstage plus tools we already run
Why this succeeds now
Proof

It is already working

The first slice — infrastructure monitoring on DX UIM — runs end to end today: request, approval, automatic activation, confirmation back. Not a concept; a working pipeline being hardened for production this quarter.

Cost

Nothing new to buy

Built from what we already run — our monitoring tools, our git server, our automation — plus open-source Backstage. The investment is engineering focus, not licenses.

Timing

AI makes this the moment

Everything is markdown and config-as-code — the medium AI tools work in natively. Bitbucket becomes the second brain of Observability, and AI adoption is safe by construction: AI proposes, humans approve, git records.

A decade of operating one way is exactly why this matters: the cost is so familiar it has become invisible. The Portal makes it visible — and then removes it. That is the fresh page.
